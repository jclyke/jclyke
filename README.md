# Jim Lyke

I'm Jim Lyke. In Brazil, people often seem to prefer James, but Jim is the name I prefer.

I'm interested in the nature and structure of computation, the ways wires interconnect, and electronics packaging. I advocate for novel concepts of modularity—including plug-and-play spacecraft and, at the farthest reaches of imagination, technologies of programmable matter.

I also enjoy studying guitar, languages, coffee, 3D printing, and puzzles. I have a mixed relationship with travel: I want neither too much nor too little.

I build small, practical software projects and use this profile as a chronological record of what I make, what I try, and what I learn—often with the assistance of AI coding tools.

Most of these projects are intentionally modest. The goal is not to make everything into a product. It is to solve useful problems, explore ideas, and preserve the progression of the work.

## Project archive

My projects use a compact date code followed by a descriptive name:

```text
jymdd-project-name
```

- `j` identifies my personal project series.
- `y` is the last digit of the year.
- `m` is the month: `1`–`9`, followed by `a` for October, `b` for November, and `c` for December.
- `dd` is the day the project began.

For example, `j6822-shadowing-looper` began on August 22, 2026.

This convention lets the repositories form a naturally chronological collection of coding experiments, AI-assisted work, and practical tools.

## Working method

My general approach is:

1. Begin with a concrete problem or curiosity.
2. Give the project a permanent date code.
3. Write down the initial intent before implementation.
4. Build the smallest useful version.
5. Test it through real use rather than treating the first design as final.
6. Preserve meaningful milestones with Git commits.
7. Keep a README that explains the purpose, decisions, and deployment.
8. Publish or share projects selectively when they may be useful to others.
